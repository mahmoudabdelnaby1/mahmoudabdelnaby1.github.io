Research Arabic Search Demand in MENA Without Treating Estimates as Facts

Keyword tools can help prioritize what to investigate, but their numbers do not describe the whole market or guarantee organic visits. For Arabic search across Egypt, Saudi Arabia, and the wider MENA region, language, spelling, dialect, product vocabulary, location settings, and the tool's measurement method all affect what appears. Build a decision from several signals and label each one accurately.

Google Keyword Planner's average monthly searches are rounded historical estimates for the selected location, network, and time range; forecasts use different inputs. Google Trends normalizes a sample of searches to a 0–100 scale for the selected time and geography. Search Console reports impressions for a site's own pages, not total market demand. These are useful but distinct measures. Keyword Planner: historical metrics and forecasts · Google Trends: how data is normalized

Define the decision before collecting volume

Start with the choice the research should inform: create a page, improve an existing page, write a product explanation, or test a paid-search message. Note the audience, market, task, language, and likely next action. “Marketing in Arabic” is too broad; “how a Saudi ecommerce team diagnoses product-feed mismatch” is a researchable task with a distinct audience and outcome.

Decide whether the job is informational, comparative, transactional, local, or support-related. Keep Arabic and English queries in separate rows even when they concern the same task; a translation does not guarantee equivalent wording or intent.

Build a bilingual query set, not a literal translation list

Collect phrases from Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools when available, customer questions, product and support language, search suggestions, and relevant query expansions. Write Arabic variants people actually use: formal Arabic, common regional wording, transliteration, English product names, and mixed-language forms. Keep the source and date for each phrase.

Do not infer demand from a phrase merely because it sounds natural to the team. Ask whether customers use the wording, inspect the results for the market, and compare whether the pages answer the same task. Group near-duplicates only when the intent and useful answer are genuinely shared.

Compare each tool within its own definition

Record the exact country, language, network, date range, match behavior, and metric definition beside every number. Keyword Planner competition concerns advertisers, not organic ranking difficulty. Its historical volume and campaign forecasts are different outputs. Google Trends is relative within the selected comparison, not a count of searches; a 100 score does not mean 100 searches, and the same score across locations does not imply equal volume.

Search Console's impressions indicate that a result from your property was shown in the report context. They do not measure all searches in the market. If two tools disagree, preserve both values and explain why their definitions differ instead of averaging them into a made-up market number.

Use a decision matrix to choose the next action

Signal What it can support What it cannot prove
Customer and sales questions Real tasks, language, and missing answers Market-wide query volume
Keyword Planner history Relative planning using stated location and period settings Organic ranking difficulty or guaranteed traffic
Google Trends Relative interest over selected times and places Absolute search counts
Search Console queries Existing visibility and clicks for this property Total demand across the market
Search-result review Page types and intent currently surfaced Future rankings or conversion rates

Choose one next action per intent cluster: update a useful page, create a genuinely distinct page, test a message, or do nothing yet. Record why and what evidence would change the decision.

Short-video script: a volume estimate is not a traffic promise

Hook: “Keyword Planner shows 1,000 searches. Does that mean your page gets 1,000 visits? No.”

Value: Explain that estimates depend on location and time; Trends is normalized; Search Console describes a site's own impressions. Show the metric label beside each number.

Close: “Write down the source and settings before choosing a topic. Save the comparison table.”

Educational carousel: five questions before trusting a keyword number

  1. Cover: What does this search-volume number actually measure?
  2. Location: Which country, network, and period did I select?
  3. Language: Is this Arabic phrase used by the audience or translated by the team?
  4. Definition: Is the value historical, forecast, normalized, or property-specific?
  5. Intent: What task does the result page satisfy?
  6. Decision: Which page or experiment will the evidence change?

Field experiment thread: compare Arabic wording in one market

1/ Select one task and one country; write down the intended audience and outcome.

2/ Collect a small query set from real questions, existing search reports, and tool suggestions. Keep formal Arabic, dialect wording, transliteration, and English product terms labeled.

3/ Compare the candidate phrases in the same tool settings and date range. Save screenshots or exports with the exact definitions.

4/ Review the result pages and ask a representative reader which wording matches their task. Do not treat a tiny sample as representative of the whole country.

5/ Choose one content action and specify what evidence would trigger a revision. Report the method and limitations, not a universal volume conclusion.

Tool-neutral prompt pack: audit a keyword evidence sheet

Prompt 1 — Classify evidence: “For each row, identify the source, geography, date range, language, metric definition, and whether it is observed or estimated. Do not infer missing settings.”

Prompt 2 — Group intent: “Group these Arabic and English queries by the user task they express. Keep phrases separate when the result type or next action differs. Flag translations that may not reflect local usage.”

Prompt 3 — Recommend a next step: “Using the supplied evidence only, recommend create, update, test, or defer for each group. Separate supporting evidence, uncertainty, and the observation that would change the decision.”

Stop-doing swipe file: replace volume myths

Stop: “Trends reached 100, so there were 100 searches.”

Use: “Trends normalized this term to 100 within the selected geography and time range.”

Stop: “Keyword Planner competition is high, so organic ranking is difficult.”

Use: “Planner's advertiser competition is high; we will separately inspect organic results and page quality.”

Stop: “Search Console shows 200 impressions, so the market has 200 searches.”

Use: “Our property recorded 200 impressions under this report's filters; the figure is not total market demand.”
