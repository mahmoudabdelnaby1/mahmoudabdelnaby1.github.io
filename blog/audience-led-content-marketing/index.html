Build Content from Audience Tasks, Not from a Topic List

Turn real audience questions into original, useful assets with evidence, distribution, and review.

Mahmoud Abdelnaby·8 min read·العربية

A list of topics is not an editorial plan. “AI,” “email,” or “SEO” names a subject; it does not specify what a reader needs to decide or do.

Start with a real audience task: compare options, diagnose a failure, complete a process, reduce a risk, or explain a result. Gather wording from support questions, sales conversations, search queries, community discussions, or product use—without exposing private information. Separate verified observations from assumptions.

For every asset, write a compact brief: 1. Reader and context. 2. Question or task to resolve. 3. One-sentence answer or promise. 4. Evidence, example, or method that makes it distinct. 5. Limits and what the material cannot prove. 6. Primary destination and useful supporting links. 7. How it will be adapted for each distribution channel. 8. A review date or condition that would make it stale.

Create one substantial source asset before deriving smaller formats. A guide can become a checklist, demonstration, email lesson, or discussion prompt—but each adaptation should fit the platform and add context. Do not publish near-identical copies just to multiply URLs. That creates editorial clutter and weakens the reader’s reason to choose one version.

A human review should check accuracy, permissions, clarity, and whether an example is real or synthetic. Label synthetic examples honestly. Measure the content’s intended job—qualified discovery, useful completion, informed contact, or reduced support friction—not just how many pieces were produced.

Arabic content needs transcreation and claim review

A literal English-to-Arabic translation can retain a metaphor that sounds unnatural or shifts the meaning. Rewrite for the target reader, then check dialect consistency and grammatical address. For claims—especially health, beauty, finance, or measurable performance—keep a source and reviewer beside every statement that could affect a decision. A synthetic scenario is useful for demonstrating a workflow only when clearly labeled; it is never a client case study or measured outcome.

Turn an interview into a publishable question

Ask customers to reconstruct the last time they tried to solve the task: what triggered it, what they searched, what they misunderstood, what they compared, and what made them trust the next step. Capture exact phrases with permission and remove identifying details. Code the notes by task stage, not by demographic stereotype. Then publish one answer that resolves a real decision and link to the next useful resource. Do not treat one interview as proof of market-wide demand; use it to form a hypothesis to check against search data and additional conversations.

Three honest hooks for one useful article

Problem hook

“Your report says 18 leads. How many were actually eligible?”

Contrast hook

“A click, a chat, and a qualified request are three different events.”

Artifact hook

“Here is a four-state worksheet to find where the contact path breaks.”

Payoff rule

Use only the opening the article genuinely answers; never imply a measured portfolio result without evidence.
