Modern Standard Arabic or Dialect? Choose Content Language by Search Intent

A practical way to choose Arabic wording by market and user intent, using reviewable search and customer signals instead of dialect assumptions or unsupported voice-search claims.

Mahmoud Abdelnaby·9 min read·العربية

Should a page use Modern Standard Arabic or a local dialect? There is no single answer for every page or market. Start with the customer’s question and the language in which they can understand the solution, then use your own site data where available. Do not assume that “voice search” means everyone searches in dialect, or that a colloquial phrase guarantees faster visibility.

Google notes that people in many countries use more than one language and that Search tries to identify the language or languages most useful for showing results. It determines page language from visible content; an HTML tag or hreflang alone does not establish the language. This matters for sites with language or regional variants, but it does not tell you which expression your specific customer uses. Start with the task, not the dialect label

Identify what the person wants to do: compare a service, solve a problem, understand a term, learn a price, or complete a step. Then write two or three ways a real customer might ask. The purpose is not to stuff variants onto a page; it is to see whether the underlying intent changes.

Illustrative furniture-store examples, not observed search data:

  • Formal Arabic: “How do I choose the right dining-table size?”
  • Conversational wording: “What size table fits six people?”
  • Purchase question: “Will a 180 cm table fit in a room that is 3 metres wide?”

The second and third questions reveal different needs: estimating seating capacity and checking fit in a particular room. A useful page answers those decisions clearly; it does not merely place the three phrases one after another to target them.

Collect language signals from reviewable sources

Search Console: If data is available, open the Performance report, filter by pages or countries, and review the queries for which your pages appeared. Compare clicks, impressions, click-through rate, and average position over a defined period. The report shows queries that generated visibility for your site; it is evidence about your site and that period, not a complete survey of market demand. Search Console Performance report.

Sales and support questions: Collect actual questions after removing personal identifiers. Record market and context, and distinguish an observed customer question from an employee’s paraphrase or an AI-generated example.

Search suggestions and related questions: Use them to gather candidate wording and follow up with the source, date, and market. Their presence does not prove volume or commercial value. Do not call an expression “the most searched” without evidence that supports that claim.

Google Trends: Use it to compare interest patterns for available terms and regions when the tool has enough data. Trends data is relative and normalized; it is not an exact search-volume count and sparse terms can be unavailable.

For each phrase, record: the wording as observed, source, date, market, page or customer context, and what it can and cannot establish. This prevents an idea from quietly turning into a false demand statistic.

Match the Arabic style to the page’s job

Use clear, accessible Arabic as the default for explanations, policies, technical guidance, and pages that serve multiple countries. Use familiar colloquial wording where it improves understanding or reflects how customers actually describe a product or problem. A service page, troubleshooting guide, checkout instruction, and social caption may need different registers.

Do not overstate dialect with slang that narrows the audience or distracts from the task. Keep technical terms consistent, and explain them once where needed. In a bilingual MENA site, localize only when the difference matters to the reader—for example, product availability, price, shipping, legal terms, service area, or a genuinely different question. Do not create a Saudi and Egyptian version merely by swapping a city name.

Google recommends distinct URLs for language versions and explains that hreflang helps identify the relationship among localized versions. But language targeting is not a substitute for useful, visibly written content in the intended language. Localized versions of your pages.

Distinguish a register difference from a different market

Make a two-column comparison before creating separate pages:

Check Same page may work when… Separate localized page may help when…
Customer task The reader is solving the same problem The decision or service differs
Product or service Availability and terms match Inventory, coverage, price, or rules differ
Wording Only the expression changes The local phrase reveals a different need
Evidence One set of facts answers both groups Each market needs its own proof or instructions
Maintenance One page can stay accurate Separate details need distinct owners and updates

If the only distinction is phrasing, one clear page can often explain the common term and include familiar alternatives naturally. Separate URLs when they provide materially different information—not to multiply near-identical pages.

Turn query evidence into useful editing

Take a real query or customer question and identify the decision behind it. Check whether the current page answers that decision near the relevant section, using accurate details. Improve the explanation, example, or next step. Do not insert the exact query repeatedly if it makes the page awkward.

Keep a small evidence note: phrase, source, market, date range, likely intent, proposed page change, and reviewer. Mark uncertain interpretation as a hypothesis. A Search Console query does not explain why someone searched; a support question does not quantify all market demand. Combine signals without pretending they have identical coverage.

Measure what changed and what remains unknown

Before editing, save the page, period, countries, queries, impressions, clicks, and relevant conversion evidence where available. After the change, compare a defined later period and note seasonality, promotions, technical releases, and other simultaneous edits. A change in impressions or clicks does not by itself prove that dialect choice caused it.

If there is no Search Console baseline yet, do useful work now: review real customer questions, keep language clear, document the hypotheses, and prepare a page-specific change. Do not invent keyword demand or claim that the page has been validated by search data.

Educational carousel: Choose page language from evidence

  1. A dialect phrase is not automatically a separate search intent.
  2. Start with the customer’s task.
  3. Separate observed wording from paraphrase and hypothetical examples.
  4. Record source, date, context, and market.
  5. Use Search Console as evidence about your own site.
  6. Keep technical and commercial facts accurate across languages.
  7. Create regional pages only when the reader needs different information.
  8. Measure the edit without claiming causation from a before-and-after alone.

Field experiment thread: Compare clarity across two Arabic registers

This proposed editorial review tests comprehension, not search ranking.

1/ Choose one customer question and a single intended task. Record where the question came from and whether it was observed or paraphrased.

2/ Draft a clear Modern Standard Arabic answer and a conversational version appropriate to the named market. Preserve the same facts and next action.

3/ Ask several intended readers, without explaining the difference, what the page helps them do and what remains unclear.

4/ Record comprehension, unfamiliar terms, and any misleading interpretation. Do not treat a small convenience sample as representative market research.

5/ Choose the clearer register or combine them carefully, then review the rendered page and regional facts.

6/ If Search Console data is available, record page/query performance before and after over comparable periods. A small comparison cannot isolate language as the cause of a search change.

Stop-doing swipe file: stop targeting every synonym in one title

  • Stop: “Website design price, website creation cost, programming prices, Egypt website quote” as one title. Use: “What changes the cost of a company website?” Then explain real cost drivers and provide a range only if documented pricing supports it.
  • Stop: “Best [service] company in every city” when coverage or superiority is unproven. Use: A page that names the cities actually served, the service process, boundaries, and a clear way to verify or contact.
  • Stop: Creating Egyptian and Saudi copies by replacing one word. Use: Localize when availability, price, terms, service, or context genuinely differs, and connect equivalent language versions correctly.
  • Stop: Calling a suggestion or one customer question “the most searched.” Use: Name the source and say what that evidence covers.

Hook–Value–Template: build a page from a customer’s words

Hook: “A difference between formal Arabic and dialect does not always mean the person wants a different answer.”

Value: Preserve the actual question, identify the task behind it, and write an answer that fits the market. Site data describes visibility for your site; support questions reveal the needs of people who contacted you. Each source has limits.

Template: “Customer question: [observed wording]. Market and context: [documented detail]. Source and date: [source]. What they need to do: [intent]. Helpful answer: [accurate steps or criteria]. Local wording that aids understanding: [term if needed]. What we still do not know: [gap].”

Illustrative example: “Question: Will this product fit a small room? Task: Compare its dimensions with available space. Answer: show actual dimensions and how to measure the room. Do not infer that the phrase has high search demand from site data alone.”
