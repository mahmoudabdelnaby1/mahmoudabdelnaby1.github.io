When Should a Business Show the Price in a Social Post?

A practical pricing-reply framework for fixed, tiered, scope-based, and temporary offers.

Mahmoud Abdelnaby·6 min read·العربية

When Should a Business Show the Price in a Social Post?

“DM us for the price” is not automatically wrong, but it can add an avoidable step when the price is fixed and the buyer needs it to compare options. A message-first quote can make sense when the price truly depends on size, scope, location, or a service assessment. The useful question is whether a customer can understand the price or pricing logic before they share details—and whether they know what will happen next.

WhatsApp Business catalogs can show product or service details, including price in supported contexts; availability varies by country. Verify the feature in the actual account and market. Classify the price before writing the reply

Fixed price: State the amount, currency, and what is included. Link to the product or checkout path.

Starting price: Say what the starting option includes and which upgrades change the total. Do not use “from” to disguise an unavailable base option.

Scope-dependent price: Explain the factors that affect the quote and provide an approved range if the business can support one. Ask only the questions that materially change the estimate.

Time-limited offer: Include dates, eligibility, products, branches, and conditions. Remove or update the offer when it ends.

If no approved price or range exists, do not invent one. Explain what assessment is needed and the next step to get a quote.

Make the first answer useful before moving to private chat

When the price is fixed, put it in the post, catalog, or product page when appropriate. Keep key details close: size, quantity, warranty, delivery, or service duration. If the price changes, update the source of truth and then check pinned posts, saved replies, ads, and linked pages.

When the price genuinely varies, explain why. “The estimate depends on the kitchen area and material; send the measurements and we’ll provide an initial range” gives a buyer more context than “message us.” State who will respond and when only if the team can honor that expectation.

Use this structure for a scoped quote: (1) direct price or reason it cannot yet be fixed; (2) what is included; (3) what changes the price; (4) the specific information needed; and (5) the next step and response expectation. Ask for personal details only when necessary, and follow the business's privacy process.

Match the response to the buyer's actual question

Prepare separate replies for price, availability, delivery, size, and service scope. A person asking “how much?” may need a direct amount, not an automated qualification sequence. If the business needs a detail, explain why it affects the quote and avoid collecting unrelated information.

Review a sample of conversations with sensitive details removed. Look for repeated follow-up questions, unanswered price requests, mismatched offer dates, and staff inventing estimates. Use this evidence to improve the post or saved reply rather than assuming every private message means higher intent.

Measure the path, not just message volume

Track the post or ad, the message start, the question type, whether a valid quote was sent, the next action, and the recorded business outcome. Define what counts as a qualified inquiry and who verifies it. A click to message is not a sale, and a conversation is not automatically a qualified lead.

Compare price-visible and message-first variants only when the offer, audience, period, and destination are sufficiently comparable. Record stock, promotion, response time, and tracking changes. Do not make a causal claim from a small or uneven sample.

Choose a response path with a simple decision table

Pricing situation Public information Private follow-up
One fixed amount Price, currency, inclusions, current availability Answer a specific fit or delivery question
Several defined tiers Starting or tier prices and differences Help choose only when the buyer requests it
Scope-based quote Factors, example range if approved, quote steps Ask the minimum details needed to estimate
Temporary offer Dates, eligibility, limitations, source page Confirm a stated condition or branch detail
Price not approved Explain when it will be confirmed and by whom Gather the precise inputs for approval

Short-video script: give the buyer a useful first answer

Hook: “If the price is fixed, why make someone ask for it?”

Value: Show two cases: a fixed product price that belongs on the page, and a scope-based quote that needs a measurement. Explain what each first reply should include.

Close: “Make the next step clear, then measure qualified conversations—not message count alone.”

Educational carousel: choose what to disclose

  1. Cover: When should a business show the price?
  2. Fixed price: state amount, currency, and inclusions.
  3. Tiered price: show the starting option and what changes it.
  4. Scope-based: explain factors and ask only necessary questions.
  5. Temporary offer: publish dates and conditions clearly.
  6. Measure quote quality and outcomes, not inbox volume alone.

Field experiment thread: compare two pricing paths fairly

1/ Select one product or service and write down the current approved price and offer conditions.

2/ Create a variant that shows the fixed price and a variant that explains the quote path. Keep the audience, destination, and period comparable where possible.

3/ Define message start, qualified inquiry, quote sent, and completed sale before the test.

4/ Record response time, availability, cancellations, and tracking gaps; these can change the result.

5/ Report the sample and observed outcome. If the variants differ in audience or offer, say the test cannot isolate price visibility.

Tool-neutral prompt pack: improve a pricing response

Prompt 1 — Classify the price: “Using only the supplied policy and product facts, classify this price as fixed, tiered, scope-based, temporary, or unapproved. Identify missing evidence; do not invent an amount.”

Prompt 2 — Draft a useful reply: “Answer the buyer's question directly in [language]. State inclusions and conditions from the source. If a quote needs more information, ask only for the details that change the estimate and explain why.”

Prompt 3 — Audit conversations: “From these anonymized conversations, group unanswered questions and unclear pricing points. Do not infer a person's income, intent, or identity. Recommend one content or process change tied to observed friction.”

Stop-doing swipe file: replace price friction

Stop: “DM for price” when the amount is fixed and approved.

Use: “Price: [amount and currency]. Includes [items]. Check availability or order here: [link].”

Stop: “Starting at [low number]” when the base option is not realistically available.

Use: “Options start at [amount] for [scope]; [factor] changes the estimate. Request a quote with [necessary input].”

Stop: “Send your number and we’ll call” before explaining why contact details are needed.

Use: “To estimate [service], we need [specific details]. Choose whether you prefer a reply here or [approved contact route].”

Hook–Value–Template: make pricing expectations clear

Hook: “Can a customer understand the price or quote path before starting a conversation?”

Value: A clear first answer reduces guesswork and lets the team focus on genuine fit questions without hiding the limits of the offer.

Template: “Price type [ ]. Amount/range and currency [ ]. Includes [ ]. Changes with [ ]. Current dates and availability [ ]. Buyer needs to provide [minimum details]. Next step [ ]. Response owner/time [verified expectation]. Measure [qualified inquiry/quote/order].”
